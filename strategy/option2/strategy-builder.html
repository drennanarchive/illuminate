Component Outline

Strategy planning terms from highest-level vision down to daily execution steps

Purpose

This resource establishes a shared vocabulary for five related strategy components, clarifies how each one serves a different purpose, and surfaces the breakdowns that commonly weaken planning.

01

Goal

The strategic vision

What it is
The broad, long-term destination your organization wants to reach.
Characteristics
High-level, aspirational, and often qualitative.
Example

“Become the most customer-centric software company in our industry.”

02

Objective

The specific target

What it is
A specific outcome that advances the goal.
Characteristics
Mid-term, clearly defined, and aligned directly with the overarching Goal.
Example

“Launch a brand new customer loyalty reward program by Q3.”

03

Key Result

The success metric

What it is
The quantifiable outcome that proves you successfully achieved your Objective.
Characteristics
  • Measurable outcomes used to determine progress toward an objective
  • Most organizations actually score Key Results
Examples
  • Increase adoption from 42% to 70%
  • Reduce cycle time from 28 days to 14 days
  • Complete 90% of modernization roadmap

Interpretation note

Even if you only reach 63%, you’ve still measured meaningful progress.

04

KPI

The health check

What it is
An ongoing operational metric that tells you how efficiently your daily business is running.
Characteristics
Tracked continuously (weekly/monthly) to monitor steady-state health and flag problems early.
Example

“Monthly Customer Churn Rate” or “Average Support Ticket Resolution Time.”

Example scenario

Objective: Improve customer retention.

Key Result: Increase retention from 78% to 88%.

KPI: Monthly churn rate.

Once you’ve achieved the objective, the KPI keeps watching to make sure you don’t backslide.

05

Milestone

The progress checkpoint

What it is
A specific, time-bound event or output (not measures) on a project schedule that shows you are moving forward.
Characteristics
Fixed deadlines, output-focused, and non-numerical (usually a “done/not done” task).
Example

“Finalize the loyalty program software vendor contract by March 15th.”

II.

How they connect

Each component answers a different strategic question. Together, they connect long-term direction to measurable progress and operational execution.

Goal tells you Why you are moving.
Objective tells you Where you are going next.
Key Result tells you If you successfully arrived.
KPI tells you How Fast/Smoothly you are driving.
Milestone tells you What Markers you passed along the way.

III.

Where organizations actually break down

Watching how organizations actually use these tools, the framework is rarely where things break down. Here’s where they do:

  1. 01

    Goals get written to sound good, not to mean something.

    “Become the most customer-centric organization in our industry” is the example above, and it’s also exactly the kind of goal that gets approved in a leadership offsite and then never touched again. If you can’t describe what not achieving it looks like, it’s not functioning as a goal—it’s functioning as a values statement wearing a goal’s clothes.

    Those two things aren’t the same, and conflating them is one of the most common strategic planning failures.

  2. 02

    Objectives get written at the wrong altitude.

    Too high and they’re just restatements of the goal. Too low and they’re tasks. The useful zone is narrow—specific enough to create focus, broad enough to allow multiple paths to success. Most organizations err toward too high because it feels safer. Vague objectives are harder to fail.

  3. 03

    Key Results get written to be achievable, not to be meaningful.

    This is the sandbagging problem. If your Key Results are things you’re already confident you’ll hit, they’re not measuring ambition—they’re measuring activity. The OKR literature (John Doerr especially) suggests that hitting 70% of a well-written Key Result is better than hitting 100% of a poorly written one. Most organizations don’t have the psychological safety to operate that way, so they quietly write easier targets.

  4. 04

    KPIs multiply until they lose meaning.

    A dashboard with 40 KPIs is not a health check—it’s noise. The discipline of KPIs isn’t in defining them, it’s in cutting them until only the ones that actually drive decisions remain. Most organizations accumulate KPIs over time and never retire them, so the dashboard grows and the signal shrinks.

  5. 05

    Milestones get treated as progress when they’re actually just schedule.

    “Milestone achieved” means the plan is intact. It says nothing about whether the work is good, whether the objective is still the right one, or whether the environment has changed since the plan was written. In fast-moving or ambiguous environments, where the Agency finds itself today, milestones can create a false sense of forward motion.

  6. 06

    The Deliverables Point, Extended

    In certain work, Deliverables are often the only thing that’s unambiguously real. Goals can shift, Objectives can be reframed, Key Results can be rescored—but a deliverable either exists or it doesn’t. That concreteness is actually an asset if you use it right. The risk is treating deliverable completion as a proxy for objective achievement, which it isn’t. You can produce every deliverable on time and still fail the mission if the deliverables were the wrong ones.

The meta-point

These frameworks are vocabulary tools first and measurement tools second.

Their primary value is forcing a team to have explicit conversations about what success looks like before the work starts—not giving leadership a dashboard to check afterward. When organizations skip the conversation and go straight to populating the template, they get the cost of the framework without the benefit.
